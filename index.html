No Leap Years


Without leap years, it might be around...


You've probably wondered what the date would be without leap years.
This website gives a rough estimate. Of course, it would be difficult to give a truly accurate date, and that's because of the history of leap years and their early adoption and utilization.


How Is This Calculated?

Leap years can be found in the ancient Roman calendar from before the Common Era. However, they did not add a single day, but an entire month. This month, Mercedonius, was said to be 27 days long. Though it was theoretically added every 2-3 years, political corruption affected its actual usage. Because the Republic was formed in ~509 BCE, I will estimate that this is when a formal calendar began usage. Of course, they probably used a formal calendar with leap years far earlier. In 45 BCE, the Julian calendar was created, and it added a leap year every four years. However, for the first few decades of use leap years came every three years. Thirteen leap years passed before they were temporarily paused to realign with the Earth's rotation. The next leap year was in 7 CE. There were 393 leap years in between 7 CE and 1582 CE in the Julian Calendar. In 1582, the Gregorian calendar was introduced to the world. It had a new way of calculating leap years. With the new rules, there are 107 leap years between 1582 and 2023.

As you can guess, while it's easy to find all the leap days in the Julian and Gregorian calendars and simply subtract them from the current date, it's more difficult to estimate a date when taking into account the pre-Julian Roman calendar.

Here is the equation I used to calculate the date without leap years:
\[ n - \left( \left( \frac{509 - 45}{2} \right) \cdot 27 + 513 \right) \] where n = current amount of days passed since 0 CE. I subtracted 45 (the year the Julian calendar was created) from 509 (around when the Republic was formed) to get the estimated number of pre-Julian years (464), then divided it by 2 to get a rough idea of how many years featured Mercedonius (232). Being 27 days long, I multiplied 232 by 27 to get 6264, then finally added that to the 513 leap days from the Julian and Gregorian calendars. 6264 is almost 17 years, while 513 is just short of a year and a half.

But Why?

Curiosity. Since I was little I wondered what the date would be without leap years. While a concrete answer is probably impossible to find, I am happy with the estimates from this calculator. I also had fun researching for this little project and learning about the history of these calendars. I hope you find it useful, or at the very least interesting, even if I can't exactly guarantee accuracy.
